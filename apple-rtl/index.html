03

Find it, fix it, prove it

Diagnose → build → measure

Most management stories give you the result and ask you to infer the method. This one has all three parts on file, in order, with dates.

Step 01 · Diagnose Find the driver

Customer satisfaction was the metric under pressure. It was not the problem. Every advisor below 80% satisfaction was also below 72% issue resolution.

Step 02 · Build Write the intervention

An original call-handling curriculum aimed at the specific behaviours that move both numbers, taken through six revisions and delivered by me.

Step 03 · Measure Let someone else score it

Four consecutive full periods, four tracked KPIs, team row against site benchmark row — in their reporting, not mine.

Step 01 — what the numbers actually said
Dots illustrative · not real advisors

Satisfaction plotted against issue resolution. The finding was that the two moved together: everyone in the low-satisfaction group was also failing to resolve issues on the call. That reframes the problem from “be warmer with customers” to “actually finish the job,” which is a coachable, teachable behaviour.

The operating routine

Recorded · my own artifacts

The results came out of an operating routine, not out of effort. The centre of it was a roster tracker that people would call a spreadsheet and that was functionally a small programme-management system.

Tracked per advisor24 fields, spanning provisioning state, enablement sessions with dates, meeting cadence and performance
Onboarding34 steps across roughly fifteen enterprise systems — a checklist I executed and tracked, not one I wrote
External stakeholders14 a store leader and an experience manager at each of the seven sites

The seven stores

My advisors kept their home stores while they worked the phones. Each one came with a store leader and an experience manager I coordinated with, in their time zone, on their schedule. This is what “distributed” actually meant week to week.

Seven stores, six markets, five states, two time zones, fourteen store-side stakeholders. Store numbers are internal, so only my own is given. How many of my advisors came from which store is not published — that is their information, not mine. Recorded · my roster tracker

  • 01
    Every coaching goal was paired with how it would be verified

    Not “improve satisfaction” but a specific behaviour, an owner, a verification method, and a follow-up date. A quality review, an observed live call, a shadowing session. It is the same structure as the business plan — cause, action, measure — applied to one person instead of a team.

  • 02
    I handed the enablement to the people who were already good at it

    Two team members who were performing strongly led workshops on case logging and on compliance. I set the targets in advance — logging from 66% to at least 80%, compliance from 87% to above 90% — and then got out of the way. Logging finished at 88.7% and compliance at 98.11%, a 12.18-point gain. Naming an internal expert and setting their success criteria is a different skill from doing the teaching yourself.

  • 03
    The team was mid-transition, not junior

    Of thirty advisors, nineteen came from sales-floor roles and had never supported the products at this technical level; eleven came from technical backgrounds. That is a skills transition, not an onboarding problem, and it is the context the benchmark numbers should be read in.

  • 04
    I verified a stated reason against the system log

    One attendance note records a lateness explained by a system update. The update had happened four days earlier. I checked rather than accepting or rejecting it, and the note records the check. Small, but it is what documentation is actually for.

A framework I used, described rather than reproduced. My performance conversations followed a fixed written shape: what we discussed, the reason you shared with me, the expectation, what you agreed to, and how we will know we were successful. I am describing the structure and nothing else. The worked example concerns a real, identifiable person and includes their performance data, so it stays private — as does every piece of my former employer's internal training material.

Scale and close-out

Measured · their forensics

Two things a hiring manager wants to know and a portfolio almost never answers: how big was it really, and what happened when it stopped.

Growth hides a lot of management problems. Contraction does not.

06

Apple North Star — R290

Recorded · written at the time

North Star, in San Antonio, is where I started in October 2016 and where I stayed. The at-home role was not a departure from it — I was still reporting to my managers there throughout, a matrix rather than a handover, and presenting the two as a sequence would be wrong. Four things from those years carry a number or a change I designed.

These come from notes I wrote at the time, not from anyone else's measurement — weaker than the benchmark figures earlier on this page, stronger than memory, and labelled accordingly. It is the store I came up in, and the one whose internal number I have given, because it is mine.

  • 01
    Repair volume kept in-house: below 60% → 70%

    I disagreed with feedback telling me to push repairs off-site. Rather than ignoring it or complying, I went and understood the reasoning first, then changed how the technical team worked: lead with ownership on out-of-warranty work, refer single-part repairs to the sister store. We hit the 60% goal inside a week and finished at 70%, the highest in the market team. I had no authority over most of the people whose behaviour changed. Recorded

  • 02
    Business engagement past 50%, from a one-line reframe

    The business-customer introduction was easy for a customer to decline, so it was rarely attempted. I reframed it as an offer to connect by email and pointed the team at a field already sitting on page one of their own workflow. Nothing was built, nothing was launched. Recorded

  • 03
    I invented a role because the workflow was breaking

    During the battery-replacement surge the volume overwhelmed how the repair room ran. I reorganised the stations, posted the relevant tips at each one, and created an unofficial “repair room captain” to hold workflow and accountability for a shift. It was not a real job title and it worked. It is also the earliest version of the same instinct that later produced the roster tracker. Recorded

  • 04
    A pilot that failed, and was right anyway

    North Star piloted fifteen-minute express appointments. It was dropped after two weeks. I kept running six to eight appointments an hour to show the model held. The company later made express appointments official for cracked-display repairs. I include this because a portfolio of only successes is not a record of judgement. Recorded

The seam between two organisations. Through the at-home period I worked directly with the store-side experience managers at all seven sites to connect two parts of the business that were not natively joined up. That is stakeholder work across an org boundary rather than management of my own team, and it is the part of this record that maps most directly onto implementation and programme roles. Recorded
07

In my own words

Each claim tagged

How I got there

In March 2020 the stores closed and I was sent home. By April or May I was doing remote technical support alongside other technical staff and team leads while the company worked out whether that was viable at all. When it proved out and the programme opened properly, I was promoted to run a team of at-home technicians — while still reporting to my store managers. Recalled · my account

I want to be careful with that paragraph, because it is the part of this page an ex-colleague could most easily object to. I am describing what I did and when I did it. I am not making a claim about the company's official programme history, I did not design the pilot, and I did not lead it. What I can stand behind is that I was sent home in March, was doing the work by spring, and was given a team when it scaled.

What I found

Satisfaction was the number under pressure, and the obvious move was to coach people to be warmer. The data said otherwise. Every advisor sitting below 80% satisfaction was also below 72% issue resolution — the dissatisfaction was not a tone problem, it was customers hanging up with the thing still broken. Recorded · my business plan

That reframing is the whole reason the rest worked. “Be nicer” is not coachable. “Confirm the issue is resolved before you close, and get the customer to agree it is” is coachable, observable, and moves both numbers at once.

What I built

An original call-handling curriculum, taken through six revisions, structured around three phases of a call and teaching each one twice — the principle first, then the actual words an advisor could use that afternoon. I wrote it and I delivered it. It is my own material rather than an adaptation of anyone's internal content, which is why I can describe it here at all. Recorded · my curriculum

What happened

Across four consecutive full periods the team came out ahead of the site benchmark on all four tracked KPIs — sixteen comparisons, sixteen wins — averaging 11.1 points better on customer-facing time, 7.4 on issue resolution, 4.2 on satisfaction, and 2.3 minutes faster on handle time. In one period the team's customer-facing time also came in above the whole organisation's average across 82 advisors, not just the site's. Measured · by Apple

And the team was not a small one. In the first period of the fiscal year I had 29 advisors — the largest team of the eight in the organisation — and stayed at or near the top as every team shrank around me. Measured · their forensics

The context that makes those figures mean something: nearly two-thirds of the team had come from sales-floor roles and had never supported the products at this technical level, and the whole operation was distributed across seven sites in five states and two time zones. There was no room to build it on a tenured, co-located team, because I did not have one.

What I would say about it in a room

The part I am proudest of is not the benchmark row. It is that the loop is closed and dated: a diagnosis that reframed the problem, an intervention built specifically against that diagnosis, and a result measured by somebody with no stake in flattering me. I have run that same loop since, on different problems, with the same three parts.

The part I would want to be asked about is the wind-down — because forecasting a decline, naming its cause, committing to a floor in writing beforehand, and then running the close-out in order is the piece of this that most resembles the actual job I am applying for.

Limits

Stated, not omitted
  • —
    No revenue claim, and no satisfaction-uplift percentage

    I previously carried two figures of that kind in my own materials. I went looking for their source, could not find one, and removed them. The benchmark tables on this page are what I can actually stand behind.

  • —
    No final scorecard for the closing period

    The full-period rollups end one period before the programme did. I have the commitment I made in advance and the roster I finished with, and not the grade.

  • —
    No individual's data, and no one's name

    The source files carry per-advisor metrics, internal identifiers, coaching plans and attendance records for real people. None of that is here, in any form, including in the one chart that shows dots.

  • —
    Nothing from the store years that carries an outside measurement

    Section 06 is entirely my own contemporaneous record. There is no third-party tracker behind it the way there is for section 03, and I have not dressed one up.

  • —
    No internal training material, in any amount

    Some of what I learned there was internal content and stays internal. Where a framework I used is publicly known, I have named it; where it was theirs, I have described what I did instead of reproducing it.
