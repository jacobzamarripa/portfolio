Turning field friction into a decision.

I authored an 11-page fleet right-sizing proposal for a joint challenge entry with a field lead at a regional fiber contractor. The proposal was selected as the winning submission.

My contributionProposal author
ScopeRequirements, sourcing & specification
Document11 pages
Decision rightsFleet Operations

Proposal author, joint entry. I had already left the contractor. My partner entered and submitted the joint entry under their name; I authored the document. Their first-person field experience in the submission belongs to them. This retrospective describes my contribution without publishing the original proposal, company photographs or personal details.

The company had already decided to replace its service vans. The question was which replacement and upfit would fit the technician’s daily work. I translated a field lead’s loadout into a decision document, then made the recommendation specific enough for fleet reviewers to evaluate.

02

Start with the work

The field lead described tools and customer equipment spread through a cargo van, repeated searching at service stops, and parking friction. The proposal treated tool access and inventory organization as design requirements.

Design storage around the daily loadout: tools, equipment and access patterns.
What I did

Elicited the daily inventory from the field lead: equipment dimensions, quantities, weights, restocking needs and tool access. Converted that information into storage zones, a workflow comparison and an itemized upfit.

What that demonstrates

Requirements elicitation, translating user needs into a specification, writing for a decision-maker and influencing a function I did not manage.

03

A specification reviewers could examine

The proposed configuration paired a compact pickup with enclosed storage, a drawer system and a mounted tube. This diagram recreates the storage logic; it is not an engineering drawing or a verified fitment.

The original budget already specified a 5 ft tube and pipe; the error was in the stated carry requirement. Historical proposal estimates, not current quotes or incurred spend. The truck, installation and a complete ownership-cost model were outside this total.

The proposal included vendor references and alternatives, a seven-factor workflow comparison, a payload budget and a three-phase implementation framework. These gave reviewers concrete assumptions to challenge. The payload budget remained incomplete because it omitted occupants.

04

Hand the decision to its owner

Proposed phase 01Pilot validation

Test one upfit with an active technician and collect workflow feedback.

Proposed phase 02Procurement setup

Fleet reviewers confirm fitment, suppliers, sourcing and installation.

Proposed phase 03Phased deployment

Fleet Operations determines timing and coordinates onboarding.

I proposed the framework. Fleet Operations retained authority over timing, vendors and execution. The proposal’s pilot plan is not evidence that those stages were carried out.

05

What happened

  • 01
    The authored proposal was selected

    The joint submission won the challenge. Selection is my first-hand account and is not independently documented here. The private proposal preserves the document content; my authorship is confirmed by my account and my partner’s acknowledgment.

  • 02
    I participated in outfitter specification meetings

    My partner and I attended meetings with vehicle outfitters to discuss the customizations. This is my first-hand account, recorded after the submission.

  • 03
    Rollout was reported, with a narrower evidence boundary

    The contractor had begun introducing replacement trucks. The vehicles were not yet outfitted to the proposal’s specifications when I recorded the update. I have no current verification of the final configuration or operational results.

Outcome boundary. Selection and participation in specification discussions are supported. Cost savings, time savings, safety improvements and fleet-wide implementation to my exact specification have not been measured or verified.
06

What I would change now

A selected proposal can still contain weak requirements and incomplete analysis. These are retrospective improvements, not methods I claim to have completed during the challenge.

  • 01
    Validate the requirement before defending the solution

    The submitted document repeatedly stated an 8–12 ft carry requirement. That was an error: the glow rods and PVC risers were approximately 5 ft. The 5 ft tube matched the actual inventory. Today I would check every dimension against the physical item and trace each requirement to its source.

  • 02
    Reconcile fit and payload before procurement

    The document used conflicting drum dimensions and omitted occupants from its payload calculation. I would measure the actual drums, test clearances and include the driver, passengers and every installed component before accepting a configuration.

  • 03
    Compare the full cost of replacement options

    The upfit budget did not establish total cost of ownership or payback. I would compare suitable replacement vans and trucks using vehicle acquisition, installation, maintenance, fuel and downtime assumptions with fleet reviewers.

  • 04
    Account for the vehicle as a workspace

    An enclosed van can provide a sheltered standing workspace. I would test whether the pickup configuration supports weather-sensitive work, including splicing, before treating storage access as the complete decision.

07

How I would validate the recommendation

The original 5–10 minutes saved per day was an estimate without a time study. I would replace it with a baseline and a matched pilot that tests the same kind of service work.

QuestionMeasure I would collect
Are tools faster to access?Park-to-first-tool time and retrieval time at comparable stops.
Does the daily inventory fit?Loadout checks, clearances and warehouse trips per week.
Does the configuration support the work?Weather interruptions, splicing setup and technician feedback.
Does the economic case hold?Fuel per mile, mileage, installation, maintenance and downtime costs.
What this says about how I work. I can turn field observations into a buildable recommendation and make its assumptions visible. My next step is to strengthen that recommendation with measured validation and a complete comparison of alternatives.
